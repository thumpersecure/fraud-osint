Cloud, Internet of Things (IoT), and mobile device ecosystems present digital forensics with problems that the field's classical acquisition-and-analysis model was never designed to solve. Where traditional computer forensics assumes an examiner can physically seize a device, write-block it, and produce a verified bit-for-bit image of a single, self-contained storage medium, modern evidence is frequently distributed across third-party data centers in multiple legal jurisdictions, generated by heterogeneous embedded devices with no standard operating system, and deliberately obscured by anti-forensic techniques designed to defeat exactly the kind of examination described in the standard digital forensic lifecycle. This article surveys the research literature on cloud forensics challenges, reviews an applied study of IoT forensic testing across common operating environments, and catalogues the anti-forensic techniques increasingly encountered by examiners — while explicitly flagging one frequently miscited claim in this literature so it is not repeated as settled fact.

1 Why Cloud Architecture Resists Forensic Examination

Classical digital forensics acquisition assumes physical or at least logical possession of a storage medium: an examiner can seize a hard drive, image it bit-for-bit, hash the image (typically with MD5 or SHA algorithms) to verify integrity, and examine the copy while the original remains sealed as evidence. Cloud computing breaks this assumption at multiple points. Data belonging to a single user or organization may be sharded, replicated, and relocated across physically distributed data centers operated by a third party, often crossing national borders in the process. The provider — not the investigator or even the account holder — controls the physical infrastructure, and standard cloud service models (infrastructure-, platform-, and software-as-a-service) each expose different, and often minimal, forensic access to the underlying storage and logging layers.

This is the starting point for the research discussed in this article: cloud infrastructure was engineered for scalability, redundancy, and cost efficiency, not for forensic readiness, and IoT devices were engineered for cheap, low-power, always-on connectivity, not for evidentiary integrity. Both gaps are increasingly exploited or exacerbated by actors who apply anti-forensic techniques on top of an already forensically hostile architecture.

2 The Zawoad and Hasan Meta-Study (2013)

Shams Zawoad and Ragib Hasan's 2013 arXiv paper, "Cloud Forensics: A Meta-Study of Challenges, Approaches, and Open Problems," is one of the most widely cited early efforts to systematically catalogue what makes cloud environments resistant to conventional forensic method. Rather than proposing a single new technique, the paper functions as a meta-study — surveying the existing cloud forensics literature to identify the recurring, unresolved problems facing investigators. Two problems recur most consistently across the challenges it catalogues:

  • Multi-tenancy. Cloud providers achieve economy of scale by running many customers' workloads on shared physical hardware and shared storage infrastructure. This means the data of an entirely uninvolved third party may occupy the same physical disk, virtual machine host, or storage array as the evidence an investigator needs to acquire. Isolating a single tenant's data for forensic imaging — without incidentally acquiring, exposing, or contaminating another customer's data — is far harder than imaging a dedicated physical drive, and providers have strong incentives (contractual, privacy-regulatory, and competitive) to resist broad forensic access precisely because of this entanglement.
  • Jurisdictional data fragmentation. Because cloud providers replicate and relocate data across geographically distributed data centers for redundancy and performance, a single account's evidence may physically reside in — or be fragmented across — multiple countries at once, each with different legal regimes governing law enforcement access, data protection, and evidentiary admissibility. An investigator operating under one jurisdiction's legal authority may have no lawful mechanism to compel production of data that happens to be stored, at a given moment, in a different jurisdiction, and the physical location of a given fragment may itself be unknown or may shift without notice.

Beyond these two headline problems, the meta-study catalogues open research problems spanning evidence acquisition (how to obtain a forensically sound image when the investigator has no physical access to the storage medium), isolation (how to separate one tenant's evidence from co-located tenants' data), and the general absence of standardized, cloud-specific forensic tooling comparable to the mature toolset available for on-premises computer forensics. The paper is explicitly framed as a survey of open problems rather than a solved methodology, and its lasting contribution is naming and organizing these challenges for the field rather than resolving them.

3 The Open Cloud Forensics (OCF) Model (2015)

Building on the 2013 meta-study, Zawoad, Hasan, and Skjellum published "OCF: An Open Cloud Forensics Model for Reliable Digital Forensics" at the IEEE 8th International Conference on Cloud Computing in 2015. Where the 2013 paper catalogued problems, the 2015 paper proposes a process model responsive to them.

The OCF model restructures the standard forensic process — familiar from the acquisition, analysis, and reporting phases used across digital forensics generally — around a distinguishing structural feature: it runs preservation as a process that operates in parallel with, rather than sequentially after, the other core stages of identification, collection, organization, presentation, and verification. This design choice is a direct response to the multi-tenancy and data-fragmentation problems identified in the 2013 meta-study: because cloud evidence can be altered, relocated, or lost through routine platform operations (auto-scaling, load balancing, data lifecycle policies) at any point during an investigation — not only before it begins — a forensic model for the cloud cannot treat preservation as a single upfront step the way traditional disk imaging does. Instead, OCF treats preservation as a continuous concern that must be maintained throughout identification, collection, organization, presentation, and verification, since cloud infrastructure can alter the state of evidence at any of those stages without investigator intervention.

The OCF model's naming choice ("Open" Cloud Forensics) also signals an aspiration toward a framework that could, in principle, be implemented across differing cloud provider architectures rather than being tied to any single vendor's platform — though the model is presented as a conceptual and procedural framework rather than a deployed, standardized tool comparable to the named commercial forensic suites used in traditional device forensics.

4 IoT Forensics: A Fragmented Device Landscape

If cloud forensics is complicated by the disappearance of a single, physically seizable storage medium, IoT (Internet of Things) forensics is complicated by the opposite problem: a proliferation of small, heterogeneous, purpose-built embedded devices — sensors, cameras, smart-home hubs, wearables, industrial controllers — each running different, often minimal or proprietary firmware, with no consistent file system, logging format, or standard forensic access interface. A smart thermostat, a networked security camera, and a fitness tracker may each store forensically relevant data (timestamps, network connection logs, sensor readings, paired-device identifiers) in entirely incompatible formats, frequently with limited onboard storage that overwrites older data quickly, and often with no vendor-supported mechanism for law-enforcement-grade forensic extraction at all.

This fragmentation means IoT forensic methodology cannot be a single universal procedure; it typically has to be tested and adapted per device class, per operating environment, and per attack or evidence-relevant scenario. This is precisely the gap addressed by the applied review discussed in the next section.

5 The Dubey, Bhatt, and Negi Review (2023)

Dubey, Bhatt, and Negi's 2023 paper, "Digital Forensics Techniques and Trends: A Review," published in the International Arab Journal of Information Technology (IAJIT) 20(4), pp. 644-654, surveys the broader landscape of digital forensics techniques and process models — including the evolution of digital-investigation process models from 1995 to 2015 and Digital Forensic Research Workshop (DFRWS) framework developments — while devoting specific attention to IoT forensics testing and to the anti-forensic techniques that increasingly frustrate examiners across both cloud and IoT contexts.

The paper's applied contribution is its documentation of context-based IoT forensic testing across three distinct operating environments, each associated with a different representative attack or evidence scenario:

EnvironmentRepresentative scenario tested
WindowsDenial-of-service activity
Ubuntu (Linux)Malware-related activity
AndroidInternal-attack activity

This tripartite testing structure reflects the practical reality that IoT ecosystems rarely run in isolation — an IoT device typically communicates with, is administered from, or is bridged through a conventional computing platform (a Windows management console, a Linux-based gateway or hub, or an Android companion app), and each of those platforms exposes forensically relevant artifacts (network logs, application data, process activity) governed by that platform's own file system and logging conventions rather than by any IoT-specific standard. Testing forensic technique separately across Windows, Ubuntu, and Android environments — rather than assuming a single unified IoT forensic procedure — is itself one of the paper's findings: IoT forensic methodology must be adapted to the specific host operating environment in which the relevant IoT traffic or artifact surfaces, because no single acquisition or analysis approach transfers cleanly across all three.

The paper also reiterates, in the IoT context, the core evidentiary-integrity requirement that runs through all of digital forensics regardless of platform: forensic imaging must produce a bit-for-bit copy of source media, verified via MD5 checksum and SHA hashing, because direct examination of original media is forensically improper — a requirement considerably harder to satisfy when the "source media" is a resource-constrained embedded device with no standard imaging interface, rather than a conventional hard drive.

6 Anti-Forensic Techniques

Alongside architectural challenges, Dubey, Bhatt, and Negi (2023) document the deliberate anti-forensic techniques sophisticated actors increasingly use to hinder both cloud and IoT investigations. Two techniques are specifically named:

  • Cryptographic obfuscation. Encrypting data, communications, or stored artifacts so that even if an investigator successfully acquires the underlying bits, the content remains unreadable without a key the investigator does not possess. Applied to cloud storage, communications between IoT devices and their controllers, or on-device data caches, cryptographic obfuscation can render an otherwise-successful acquisition forensically useless without a parallel decryption capability or legal compulsion to obtain the key.
  • IP and MAC address spoofing. Falsifying the network-layer (IP) or hardware-layer (MAC) address associated with a device or connection, so that network logs, connection records, and device-attribution evidence point to an incorrect or untraceable origin. This directly undermines a common IoT and network forensic technique — using logged addresses to attribute a given action or connection to a specific device or actor — and is particularly damaging in IoT contexts where many devices communicate autonomously and continuously, generating large volumes of address-based log data that investigators depend on for reconstruction.

The paper frames both techniques as part of a broader trend: as digital forensic methodology becomes more standardized and more widely understood, sophisticated adversaries adapt specifically to defeat it, meaning the "arms race" dynamic long familiar from traditional cybersecurity (attacker technique versus defensive countermeasure) now applies equally to the evidentiary layer of an investigation, not just to the initial intrusion or fraud itself. The review's broader recommendation — that investigations be conducted live, recorded, and independently verified by multiple teams — is presented as a partial institutional countermeasure to anti-forensic technique: independent, redundant verification makes it harder for a single point of evidence manipulation or spoofing to go undetected.

7 Mobile Forensics and the Cloud/IoT Convergence

Mobile devices sit at the intersection of the cloud and IoT challenges described above, compounding both. A modern smartphone is simultaneously a personal computing device with substantial local storage (subject to conventional mobile forensic extraction using commercial platforms) and a permanently connected client to numerous cloud services (messaging, backup, location, health, and third-party application data that may reside primarily or exclusively on provider servers rather than on the device itself) and a hub coordinating an increasing number of paired IoT devices (wearables, smart-home accessories, vehicle systems). An investigator who extracts only the device's local storage — even a forensically sound physical extraction — may recover a fraction of the evidentiary picture if the more evidentially significant data lives in the associated cloud account or on a paired IoT device governed by entirely separate access, jurisdictional, and anti-forensic considerations.

This convergence is a central reason the standard digital forensic lifecycle — acquisition, extraction, analysis, and reporting — cannot be treated as complete once a single device has been imaged; a thorough investigation increasingly requires coordinated acquisition strategies spanning device, cloud account, and any paired IoT ecosystem, each governed by different legal process requirements and different technical acquisition methods.

8 Blockchain Chain-of-Custody: A Citation Caution

Discussions of emerging digital-evidence integrity techniques — particularly relevant to cloud and IoT evidence, where the traditional physical chain-of-custody model (a sealed evidence bag, a signed transfer log) does not map cleanly onto distributed, provider-held, or ephemeral device data — frequently reference the use of blockchain's tamper-evident, distributed-ledger properties to create an immutable record of evidence handling from initial acquisition through court presentation.

A specific citation attributed to "Agarwal (2021)" on blockchain-based digital forensics chain-of-custody circulates in secondary literature on this topic. That citation could not be located or confirmed in any index or publisher database after multiple targeted searches, and it should be treated as unverified — and possibly a fabricated or misattributed reference. Readers and editors of this wiki should not cite "Agarwal (2021)" as a confirmed source on blockchain chain-of-custody. Separately, a real researcher named Udit Agarwal has published on a related but distinct topic — blockchain and cryptocurrency-fraud investigation (see Cryptocurrency Tracing) — in a different publication and different year than the unverified citation, which appears to be the likely source of the conflation.

What is genuinely documented and verifiable in this space are two peer-reviewed papers that should be cited in place of the unverified reference:

  • Chopade, Khan, Shaikh, and Pawar, "Digital Forensics: Maintaining Chain of Custody Using Blockchain" (2019 IEEE I-SMAC). This paper applies blockchain's distributed-ledger structure to the evidence chain-of-custody problem, proposing a tamper-evident record of each transfer, access, and handling event an item of digital evidence undergoes.
  • Lone and Mir, "Forensic-chain: Blockchain based digital forensics chain of custody with PoC in Hyperledger Composer" (Digital Investigation, 2019). This paper presents a proof-of-concept implementation built on Hyperledger Composer, demonstrating a working blockchain-based custody record rather than a purely conceptual proposal.

The underlying concept these two verified papers address — using an immutable, distributed ledger to record evidence handling so that any unauthorized alteration or gap in custody is cryptographically detectable — is a genuine and active research direction, directly relevant to cloud and IoT evidence where conventional physical custody controls are often unavailable. It should be discussed and cited using Chopade et al. (2019) and Lone & Mir (2019), not the unverified "Agarwal (2021)" reference.

9 Why This Matters for Interoperability Standards

The cloud and IoT challenges surveyed above compound a separate, closely related problem: as evidence sources multiply across cloud providers, IoT device classes, and mobile platforms, examiners increasingly need to combine, cross-reference, and validate data produced by different tools and different provider ecosystems within a single investigation. This is precisely the interoperability problem that data standards such as CASE (Cyber-investigation Analysis Standard Expression) and UCO (Unified Cyber Ontology) are designed to address — providing a common representation for observable objects, provenance, and chain-of-custody information regardless of which tool or platform originally produced the data. See Data Standards for Cyber-Investigation for a full treatment of CASE, UCO, and the related process-oriented WANDA schema, including the documented limitation that CASE is "artefact-oriented" and does not yet fully capture the process-level detail (how a given tool's algorithm transformed raw cloud or IoT data into a reported artifact) that legal reliability standards increasingly demand.

As cloud and IoT evidence sources proliferate, the need for a common, process-transparent representation format becomes more urgent rather than less: an investigator reconstructing a timeline from a cloud provider's server logs, a smart-home hub's local cache, and a paired smartphone's extraction cannot rely on any single vendor's proprietary output format to reconcile all three sources into one defensible narrative.

10 Synthesis

Cloud, IoT, and mobile forensics share a common underlying dynamic: each domain was engineered around goals — scalability, low cost, ubiquitous connectivity — that are fundamentally in tension with forensic readiness, and each is increasingly targeted by anti-forensic techniques specifically designed to exploit that tension. Zawoad and Hasan's 2013 meta-study named the structural cloud problems (multi-tenancy, jurisdictional fragmentation) that have still not been fully resolved by the 2015 OCF model's parallel-preservation approach. Dubey, Bhatt, and Negi's 2023 review demonstrates empirically that IoT forensic methodology must be tailored to the specific host operating environment (Windows, Ubuntu, Android) in which relevant artifacts surface, and documents cryptographic obfuscation and IP/MAC spoofing as concrete anti-forensic techniques investigators now routinely encounter. Mobile devices sit at the convergence point of both problems, requiring coordinated acquisition across device, cloud account, and paired IoT ecosystem. And as with several other areas of the digital forensics literature, this domain is not immune to citation error: the persistent but unverifiable "Agarwal (2021)" blockchain chain-of-custody reference should be replaced, in any rigorous discussion, with the genuinely documented Chopade et al. (2019) and Lone & Mir (2019) papers. Investigators working across these domains should treat every acquisition claim — proprietary tool output, cloud provider log, or blockchain-anchored custody record alike — against the same foundational-validity standard applied throughout digital forensics generally, as discussed in the broader Digital Forensic Lifecycle article.
