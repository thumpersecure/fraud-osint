Digital forensics (also called computer forensics in its narrowest sense) is the branch of forensic science concerned with the identification, collection, preservation, analysis and presentation of data held on digital devices and networks, carried out in a manner that keeps the evidence admissible in legal or regulatory proceedings. In fraud and financial-crime investigation it supplies much of the underlying record: emails, accounting files, transaction logs, deleted documents, chat histories and metadata that corroborate or refute allegations. Because digital evidence is fragile and easily altered, the discipline is defined less by clever recovery techniques than by rigour and documentation — the ability to show a court exactly what was found, where, and that it was not changed in the process.[1]

Digital forensics is a companion discipline to forensic accounting and open-source intelligence: forensic accountants interpret the financial substance of recovered records, while OSINT practitioners work from publicly available data. Digital forensics, by contrast, typically works from data lawfully seized or lawfully accessed, and is bound tightly to the chain of custody and to the rules of evidence and testimony.

1 The four phases

Most digital-forensic models, and the ISO/IEC standards that formalise them, describe an investigation as a sequence of four phases. The order matters: an error in an early phase — an unrecorded seizure, an unverified copy — can render everything that follows inadmissible.[2]

1.1 Collection (identification and acquisition)

The first phase is to identify potential sources of evidence — computers, phones, servers, cloud accounts, removable media, network appliances — and to acquire the data from them. Investigators must distinguish between volatile data, which is lost when a device is powered down (running processes, network connections, encryption keys and the contents of memory), and non-volatile data on disks and flash storage. Because seizure itself can alter a device, every action is logged: who did what, when, and with which tool.

1.2 Preservation

Once acquired, evidence must be preserved unchanged. This means creating a verified forensic copy, storing originals securely, and documenting an unbroken chain of custody. All subsequent examination is performed on working copies, never on the original media.

1.3 Analysis

Analysis is the interpretive phase — recovering deleted files, reconstructing timelines, searching for keywords, correlating artefacts across devices, and extracting the specific facts relevant to the investigation. It is the phase most visible to the public but, procedurally, the least distinctive: its value depends entirely on the integrity established earlier.

1.4 Presentation

Finally, findings are documented in a report and, where necessary, presented as testimony. The presentation must translate technical findings into terms a trier of fact can follow, and must survive scrutiny under the applicable admissibility standards.

2 Forensic imaging and write-blocking

A forensic image is a bit-by-bit (sector-by-sector) copy of a storage device — not a file copy. Unlike an ordinary backup, which copies only visible files, a bit-stream image captures every sector, including unallocated space, file slack, and the residue of deleted files. This is what makes recovery of deleted material possible.[3]

To guarantee the original is not modified during imaging, examiners use a write-blocker — a hardware or software device that permits reads from the suspect drive but physically or logically blocks any write. Because merely mounting a modern operating-system drive can update access timestamps and journal files, imaging without a write-blocker risks changing the very evidence being preserved, and can supply an opposing party with grounds to challenge admissibility.

3 Hashing and integrity verification

Integrity is proven mathematically through cryptographic hashing. A hash function (historically MD5, and now SHA-1 or the SHA-2 family such as SHA-256) reduces an arbitrary amount of data to a fixed-length value; changing even a single bit produces a wildly different hash. An examiner hashes the original media, hashes the forensic image, and shows the two values match — demonstrating that the copy is faithful. The hash is recorded again at each later stage, so any tampering is detectable.[3]

MD5 and SHA-1 are now considered cryptographically weak against deliberate collision attacks, and modern practice favours SHA-256. For the routine purpose of detecting accidental change or corruption, however, older hashes remain in wide use, and many tools compute more than one hash simultaneously (general knowledge). The recorded hash values are a central part of the chain of custody.

4 Analysis techniques

Several recurring techniques appear across almost every digital-forensic examination:

  • Data carving — recovering files from unallocated space by recognising file signatures (header and footer byte patterns) rather than relying on the file system's directory. Carving can recover documents and images long after they were "deleted", because deletion usually only unlinks the file rather than overwriting its contents.[4]
  • Keyword and search analysis — indexing the entire image and searching for names, account numbers, phrases, or regular-expression patterns (for example, patterns matching credit-card or SSN formats) relevant to the allegations.
  • Timeline analysis — reconstructing a chronological sequence of events from file timestamps, log entries, browser history, and system artefacts, so that activity can be placed at a particular time and often attributed to a particular user account.
  • Artefact analysis — interpreting operating-system, browser, and application traces (registry entries, link files, cache, and logs) that reveal what a user did.

In fraud work these techniques feed directly into the analytical methods of forensic accounting and, when large financial data sets are recovered, into tests such as Benford's Law.

5 Sub-fields

Digital forensics has diversified into a set of overlapping specialisms, each with its own tools and pitfalls.[4]

Sub-fieldFocusTypical evidence
Computer forensicsDesktops, laptops, servers, storageDocuments, deleted files, OS artefacts
Mobile forensicsPhones and tabletsMessages, call logs, location data, app data
Cloud forensicsRemote hosted services and accountsWebmail, storage, SaaS logs; jurisdiction issues

Memory forensicsVolatile RAMRunning processes, keys, injected malware
Malware forensicsMalicious codeBehaviour, indicators of compromise, attribution

Cloud and mobile forensics are the fastest-changing areas: cloud data may sit in another country's jurisdiction and cannot simply be imaged, while mobile operating systems encrypt storage by default, so acquisition increasingly depends on vendor tools and on lawful access to the device while unlocked. Memory forensics has grown in importance because sophisticated intrusions and some financial malware operate largely in RAM and leave little on disk.

6 Standards and frameworks

To keep results defensible and reproducible, practitioners follow published standards rather than ad-hoc method:

  • ISO/IEC 27037:2012 — international guidance on the identification, collection, acquisition and preservation of digital evidence, emphasising three qualities an investigation must demonstrate: auditability, repeatability and reproducibility.[5]
  • NIST SP 800-86 — the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology's Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response, which frames the process as collection, examination, analysis and reporting.[2]
  • SWGDE — the Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence, which publishes best-practice documents widely cited in U.S. courts.[5]

Adherence to these standards is also what allows expert testimony about digital evidence to satisfy the reliability requirements discussed in Evidence and Testimony, including the amended Federal Rule of Evidence 702.

7 Limitations

Digital forensics is powerful but not infallible. Encryption may make data unrecoverable without a key; anti-forensic techniques can wipe or falsify artefacts; timestamps can be manipulated; and attribution of activity to a specific person (as opposed to an account or device) often requires corroborating evidence. Cloud and cross-border data raise legal-access questions that technique alone cannot resolve. For these reasons digital-forensic findings are usually presented as one strand of evidence, woven together with financial analysis, documentary review and interviews, rather than as standalone proof.
