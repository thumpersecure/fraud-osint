The U.S. federal financial crime enforcement agencies are the primary government bodies responsible for investigating and prosecuting corporate fraud, tax crimes, money laundering, and securities violations in the United States. Three agencies form the operational core of this enforcement architecture: the Federal Bureau of Investigation's (FBI) White-Collar Crime program, the Internal Revenue Service's Criminal Investigation division (IRS-CI), and the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Division of Enforcement. Each operates under a distinct statutory mandate — criminal investigation, tax-code enforcement, and civil securities regulation, respectively — but the three routinely coordinate through joint task forces, given that most large-scale financial fraud implicates criminal fraud statutes, tax law, and securities law simultaneously.[1][2][3]

1 Overview

Federal financial crime enforcement in the United States is distributed across agencies with different legal tools and burdens of proof rather than concentrated in a single body. The FBI investigates federal crimes and refers cases for criminal prosecution by the Department of Justice; IRS-CI is the only federal agency empowered to investigate criminal violations of the Internal Revenue Code; and the SEC pursues civil enforcement actions (and refers criminal matters to the Department of Justice) against violations of federal securities law.[1][2][3] This division of labor means a single fraud scheme — for example, a public company that falsifies its books, evades tax on the resulting illicit gains, and launders the proceeds — can trigger simultaneous FBI, IRS-CI, and SEC involvement, each pursuing a different legal theory against the same underlying conduct. Historically documented cases such as Enron and WorldCom illustrate this multi-agency pattern, where the SEC filed civil fraud actions while criminal charges proceeded separately through the Department of Justice.

2 FBI White-Collar Crime Program

The FBI's White-Collar Crime program sits within its Criminal Investigative Division and covers a broad jurisdictional portfolio: corporate fraud, securities and commodities fraud, health care fraud, mortgage and financial institution fraud, money laundering, public corruption, and intellectual property theft. The Bureau is the lead federal agency for investigating corporate fraud and health care fraud specifically.[1]

2.1 The Placement-Layering-Integration Model

In its public-facing guidance, the FBI frames money laundering — a crime frequently intertwined with the frauds it investigates — through the classic three-stage model used across the anti-money-laundering (AML) field:

  • Placement — introducing illicit cash or proceeds into the legitimate financial system (for example, depositing criminally derived funds into a bank account).
  • Layering — moving funds through a series of transactions (wire transfers, shell companies, cross-border transfers) designed to obscure their illicit origin and break the audit trail.
  • Integration — reintroducing the now-laundered funds into the economy as apparently legitimate assets, such as real estate, businesses, or investments.

This model underlies both FBI investigative technique and the broader statutory framework discussed in AML Statutory Framework, including the Bank Secrecy Act and the Money Laundering Control Act of 1986 (18 U.S.C. §§ 1956–1957).[1]

2.2 Task Force Partnerships and IC3 Referrals

The FBI's White-Collar Crime program partners with the SEC and IRS through joint task forces on complex fraud matters. For consumer-facing fraud complaints — romance scams, business email compromise, cryptocurrency fraud, and similar online-enabled crime — the Bureau operates the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) as an intake and referral mechanism, routing complaints toward appropriate investigative units. IC3 referrals function as an early-warning and case-aggregation tool, allowing the Bureau to identify patterns across large numbers of individually small-dollar complaints that might otherwise never surface as a prosecutable case.[1]

3 IRS Criminal Investigation (IRS-CI)

IRS Criminal Investigation is the law-enforcement arm of the Internal Revenue Service and holds a jurisdictional distinction shared by no other federal agency: it is the sole federal authority empowered to investigate criminal violations of the Internal Revenue Code. Its mandate also extends to closely related financial crimes, including money laundering, Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) violations, and terrorist financing.[2]

3.1 History and the Capone Precedent

IRS-CI's investigative approach is most famously associated with the prosecution of organized-crime figure Al Capone, who was convicted on federal tax charges after evading direct prosecution for the underlying violent and racketeering crimes attributed to his organization. This historical episode established a durable enforcement template still cited today: tax-code violations can serve as a prosecutable proxy when the substantive criminal conduct (narcotics trafficking, organized crime, fraud) is otherwise difficult to prove beyond a reasonable doubt.[2]

3.2 Scale and Structure

IRS-CI employs approximately 3,000 employees, of whom roughly 2,100 are special agents, distributed across 20 domestic field offices plus roughly a dozen international attaché posts that support cross-border financial-crime investigations. The division reports a federal conviction rate exceeding 90 percent — a figure IRS-CI attributes to the rigor of its investigative and case-referral process, since cases are typically well-documented through financial records before being referred for prosecution.[2]

3.3 FY2025 Enforcement Results

In fiscal year 2025, IRS-CI identified financial crimes totaling approximately $10.59 billion, a 15.7 percent increase over the figure identified in FY2024. This scale of identified financial crime reflects IRS-CI's expanding role not only in traditional tax-evasion cases but in complex financial-crime investigations that intersect with money laundering, cryptocurrency-based fraud, and organized fraud schemes — the same terrain covered by Financial Statement Fraud and the broader AML statutory framework.[2]

4 SEC Division of Enforcement

The Securities and Exchange Commission's Division of Enforcement investigates violations of federal securities laws and litigates civil enforcement actions against individuals and entities. The Division makes up roughly one-quarter of total SEC staff, reflecting enforcement's centrality to the agency's overall mission of protecting investors and maintaining fair, orderly securities markets.[3]

4.1 The Financial Reporting and Accounting Unit

In August 2026, the SEC announced the creation of a new Financial Reporting and Accounting Unit within the Division of Enforcement — the first specialized unit dedicated solely to accounting and financial-reporting fraud and related auditor misconduct. The unit is staffed by both attorneys and accountants, a structural choice that reflects the reality that detecting and proving financial-statement fraud requires forensic-accounting expertise (the kind of transaction-level and journal-entry analysis discussed in Financial Statement Fraud) alongside legal enforcement capability. The unit's creation signals renewed institutional emphasis on accounting-fraud enforcement as a distinct enforcement priority, separate from the SEC's broader securities-fraud and market-manipulation caseload.[3]

4.2 Civil Enforcement Tools

Unlike the FBI and IRS-CI, which pursue criminal investigations that can result in imprisonment, the SEC's enforcement authority is fundamentally civil: it can bring administrative proceedings and federal civil actions seeking remedies such as disgorgement of ill-gotten gains, civil monetary penalties, and injunctions barring individuals from serving as officers or directors of public companies. Where SEC investigators uncover conduct that also appears criminal, the Commission refers those findings to the Department of Justice for potential criminal prosecution — a pattern documented in major cases discussed in Major Fraud Case Studies, where SEC civil actions against corporate officers ran in parallel with separate criminal prosecutions of the same individuals.[3]

5 Interagency Coordination

Because financial fraud schemes routinely violate criminal fraud statutes, the tax code, and securities law simultaneously, the FBI, IRS-CI, and SEC frequently coordinate on the same underlying conduct rather than operating in isolation. Joint task forces allow the agencies to share financial-transaction evidence, deconflict overlapping investigations, and sequence civil and criminal actions so that one does not compromise the other (for example, delaying a civil SEC filing until a parallel criminal investigation reaches an appropriate stage). This coordination model mirrors the placement-layering-integration framework's implicit assumption that illicit financial conduct crosses institutional and jurisdictional lines, which is why no single agency's authority is sufficient on its own to fully address large-scale financial crime.[1][2][3]

6 Relevance to Fraud Examination and OSINT

For open-source investigators and forensic accountants, understanding this three-agency structure clarifies where different categories of evidence and allegation are likely to be actionable. A suspected tax-evasion scheme uncovered through OSINT research on shell companies or offshore transfers falls within IRS-CI's exclusive jurisdiction; suspected market manipulation, insider trading, or falsified public-company financial statements fall within the SEC's civil enforcement remit and, increasingly, the new Financial Reporting and Accounting Unit's specific focus; and broader fraud schemes involving health care billing, mortgage fraud, or public corruption are more likely to fall under the FBI's White-Collar Crime program. Recognizing which agency's mandate a given fact pattern implicates — and that a single complex scheme may implicate all three — is a foundational step in directing a complaint, tip, or referral to the body with actual jurisdiction to act on it.
