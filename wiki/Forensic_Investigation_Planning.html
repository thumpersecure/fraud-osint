Forensic Investigation Planning

From the Fraud-OSINT Wiki, the free forensic-accounting & OSINT reference
Forensic Investigation Planning
PurposeTurn an allegation into a defensible, proportionate, evidence-led investigation
Core methodFraud theory approach (hypothesis, test, refine)
TriggerPredication: a reasonable basis to believe fraud has occurred, is occurring or will occur
Key legal anchorsUpjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981); work-product doctrine
Governance frameIIA Three Lines Model (2020)
Typical outputsScope memo, evidence plan, chronology, findings report, remediation plan

Forensic investigation planning is the disciplined front end of a fraud examination: the decisions made between the moment an allegation arrives and the moment the first record is pulled. Poorly planned investigations tend to fail in recognisable ways. They tip off the suspect, destroy privilege, wander into data nobody can use in court, or answer a question the client never asked. Well planned ones apply the same steps whether the matter is a $40,000 vendor kickback or a restatement at a listed company: establish predication, frame hypotheses, secure and preserve evidence, test hypotheses against records, and report conclusions that a finder of fact can follow. This article covers the engagement methodology as taught in forensic-accounting programmes and practised by corporate investigators; techniques for the later stages are developed in Forensic Accounting, Interviewing and Report Writing.

1 Predication and the allegation response plan

An examination should not begin on a hunch, a grudge or curiosity. ACFE methodology requires predication, the totality of circumstances that would lead a reasonable, professionally trained and prudent person to believe a fraud has occurred, is occurring or will occur.[1] Allegations arrive through several channels. In the ACFE's 2026 study of 2,402 cases from 143 countries, 43% of frauds were detected by tip, and more than half of those tips came from employees; internal audit (15%) and management review (13%) were the next most common routes.[2] The intake process is therefore a control in its own right. Section 301 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires audit committees of listed companies to establish procedures for receiving, retaining and treating complaints about accounting, internal-control and auditing matters, including confidential anonymous submissions by employees, and the same logic underlies the whistleblower regimes that reward reporting.

A written allegation response plan (sometimes called a fraud response plan) fixes in advance who does what in the first 48 hours. A typical plan contains:

  • Triage criteria — who logs the allegation, how credibility and severity are rated, and which allegations (senior-management involvement, regulatory exposure, financial reporting impact) bypass management and go straight to the audit committee or board.
  • Conflict screening — ensuring nobody in the reporting line of the accused, and no person named in the allegation, receives case information or directs the work.
  • Preservation notices — legal-hold instructions covering email, chat platforms, shared drives, mobile devices, badge logs and accounting-system audit trails, issued before the subject learns of the inquiry. Auto-delete settings on messaging applications are a frequent point of failure; see Chain of Custody and Digital Forensic Lifecycle.
  • Containment decisions — whether to suspend system access, change approval limits or place the subject on leave, weighed against the risk of tipping off and of wrongful-accusation claims.
  • Escalation and disclosure triggers — thresholds at which regulators, insurers, lenders or the external auditor must be told (see section 8).
  • Whistleblower protection — a commitment against retaliation, which is also a legal duty under several statutes.

Triage is not a verdict. The purpose is to decide, quickly and on the record, whether the allegation is specific enough to test (a named process, period and mechanism) or whether it needs a preliminary, low-footprint review of readily available data before the organisation commits to a full investigation.

2 The fraud theory approach

The ACFE's fraud theory approach is a hypothesis-driven loop modelled on the scientific method, in which the examiner (1) analyses the available data, (2) creates a hypothesis, usually a worst-case scenario naming a possible scheme, perpetrator, method and timeframe, (3) tests the hypothesis with if-then reasoning, and (4) refines or amends it as evidence accumulates, repeating the cycle.[1] The if-then step is where planning becomes concrete: if a warehouse clerk is diverting goods, then the shipping logs and the perpetual-inventory records should show a traceable discrepancy; if a purchasing manager has a conflict of interest, then a vendor's address, bank account or registered owner should connect to him or her.

Three disciplines keep the approach honest. First, the examiner states hypotheses that can be falsified, and deliberately seeks exculpatory as well as inculpatory evidence; an examiner never opines on guilt, which is for the trier of fact. Second, hypotheses are ranked by likelihood and by cost of testing, so cheap, high-yield tests (a vendor-master match against employee addresses, a duplicate-payment query) run first. Third, the working hypothesis list is written down and dated, which later documents that alternative explanations were considered. The approach pairs naturally with the fraud tree, whose branches supply candidate schemes, and with the red flags that suggest which branch to test first. When the question is how much was taken rather than whether, the net worth method and bank statement analysis become the primary tools.

3 Scoping, team and privilege

Scope

The scope memo defines the question, the period, the entities, the people in and out of scope, the deliverable and the stopping rule. Scope is deliberately staged: a first phase confirms or rejects the core allegation using a bounded set of records, and expansion (additional entities, longer periods, additional custodians) is authorised only when phase-one results justify it. Open-ended scope is the main driver of runaway cost. Engagement letters should also record who the client is (the board, the audit committee, or outside counsel), who may direct the work, what the examiner will not do (for example, provide legal conclusions), and how documents will be retained.

Team

Teams are assembled for the specific hypotheses. Common members are outside counsel; forensic accountants; a digital forensics specialist who can image devices and mailboxes; data analysts who can query ERP and bank data; an investigator or open-source intelligence analyst for public-record and background work (see Public Records and Beneficial Ownership); and, where relevant, subject-matter experts such as engineers or actuaries. Independence matters: the external auditor generally should not be the investigator of its own client's possible fraud, and the people who designed or operated the suspect process should not be the ones to investigate it. Professional-conduct obligations are covered in Forensic Accountant Ethics.

Privilege

Privilege decisions are made at the start, because they cannot be repaired later. In Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981), the Supreme Court held that the attorney-client privilege protected communications between a company's counsel and employees outside the "control group" made during an internal investigation of questionable foreign payments, and that work-product protection applied to counsel's interview notes.[3] The practical consequences are routine: counsel retains the forensic accountants (the approach associated with United States v. Kovel, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961), under which an accountant assisting a lawyer to give legal advice can fall within the privilege), the engagement letter recites that the work is performed to assist counsel in rendering legal advice, interviewers give Upjohn warnings that counsel represents the company and not the individual, and findings are routed to counsel before circulation. Privilege can be lost by sharing work product too widely or by later disclosing it to regulators, so the decision to cooperate with an agency must be taken with that trade-off in view. Privilege rules differ by jurisdiction, and in some countries in-house counsel communications receive weaker protection; local advice is essential in cross-border matters.

4 The evidence collection plan

The evidence collection plan maps each hypothesis to the records that would confirm or refute it, the custodian of each record, the method of acquisition and the order of collection. A useful structure is a matrix with one row per record set and columns for source, custodian, format, legal authority or consent, preservation method, volatility and priority. Planning principles include:

  • Order of volatility. Collect the data most likely to disappear first: mobile devices, cloud-chat retention, system logs with short rolling windows, CCTV, then email archives, then paper and archival ledgers.
  • Forensic copies, not working originals. Mailboxes and devices are imaged by a specialist and hashed; analysis runs on copies. See Digital Forensics and Digital Forensics Tools.
  • Primary over derivative. Prefer bank-supplied statements and third-party confirmations to company-prepared schedules. Where the suspect controls the schedule, treat it as an assertion to be tested.
  • Structured data first, documents second. Full-population extracts of the general ledger, journal-entry detail with user IDs and timestamps, vendor master, payroll master and bank transactions allow whole-population tests (see Fraud Detection Analytics and Benford's Law) rather than samples.
  • Third-party and public sources. Registry filings, court dockets, property records and web archives (see Web Archives) are collected without alerting the subject and often establish the link between an insider and a counterparty.
  • Interview sequencing. Witnesses with least involvement are interviewed first, the subject last, once the documentary picture is fixed. See Interviewing.
  • Chain of custody. Every transfer is logged from the outset so that admissibility is not an afterthought (Chain of Custody).

Cross-border collection adds data-protection and blocking-statute constraints: personal data may not lawfully be exported or even reviewed in some jurisdictions without a legal basis, so the plan must record the authority for each transfer.

5 Adaptive process analysis

Forensic curricula often describe the working method of a complex investigation as adaptive process analysis: rather than executing a fixed audit programme, the team maps the business process under review (order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, hire-to-retire), identifies where a motivated insider could subvert each step, tests those points, and re-draws the map as the evidence reveals how the process actually operated as opposed to how the manual says it should. The term is a teaching label rather than a codified standard; its substance is the iterative refinement already built into the fraud theory approach, applied to process flows.

A typical cycle proceeds as follows:

  1. Document the designed process from policies, system configuration and walkthroughs, noting each approval, reconciliation and segregation-of-duties point.
  2. Observe the actual process through transaction-level data. Where the data shows approvals by the same user who created a record, payments just below an approval threshold, or journal entries posted outside business hours, the actual process has diverged from the design.
  3. Identify the override or bypass that explains the divergence: a shared credential, a manual journal route, an exception workflow, a vendor flagged as "one-time".
  4. Re-scope the tests around the discovered bypass, for example extending from one approver to all transactions that used the same route.
  5. Quantify exposure across the full population once the pattern is stable, rather than extrapolating from the first case found.

The method is especially valuable in procurement and financial-reporting matters, where the fraud is often a systematic use of a legitimate-looking exception path rather than a single forged document. It also feeds control remediation, because the same map that located the bypass identifies the control that should have closed it (Internal Controls and Fraud Risk Assessment).

6 The timeline "time machine": scheme versus controls

Investigators often build a master chronology that overlays two tracks on a single time axis, informally a "time machine" that lets the team step back to any date and ask what was happening on both sides:

TrackEvents recordedSources
Scheme trackFirst anomalous transaction, escalation steps, changes in method, inflection points in dollar volume, communications between participants, subject's known pressures or milestonesGL and bank data, email and chat metadata, travel and expense records, public records
Control trackPolicy changes, approval-limit changes, staffing and role changes, system migrations or upgrades, audit and review dates, turnover of reviewers, vacation of the key person, management-override opportunitiesPolicy versions, ERP change logs, HR records, internal-audit and external-audit reports, access-rights histories
Context trackEarnings targets, covenant tests, bonus plan triggers, budget cycles, litigation or regulatory eventsBoard minutes, bonus plans, debt agreements, press and filings

The overlay answers questions the transaction data alone cannot. Did the scheme begin the week an approval limit was raised, or after the only reviewer left? Did it stop on the date the subject went on leave (evidence for the identity of the perpetrator) or on the date an audit was announced (evidence of knowledge)? Did the amounts spike in the final fortnight of each quarter, pointing to an earnings or covenant motive, as in the cases summarised in Financial Fraud Case Studies in Detection? Aligning the chronology with the fraud triangle also lets the team identify the likely pressure and opportunity windows. Each entry should be tied to a source document so that the chronology can be handed to counsel as an evidence index and, if needed, to an expert witness.

7 Roles under the Three Lines Model

The Institute of Internal Auditors updated its model in July 2020, renaming the "Three Lines of Defense" the Three Lines Model and giving greater emphasis to governance and value creation rather than defence alone.[4] In the updated model, the governing body (board or audit committee) is accountable for oversight and sets the organisational direction; management owns actions to achieve objectives and manage risk (first-line roles deliver products and services; second-line roles provide expertise, support and monitoring on risk management and compliance); and internal audit, as the third line, provides independent and objective assurance and advice. Both management and internal audit report to and are overseen by the governing body.

For investigations, the model clarifies who may do what:

  • The first line (process owners) often holds the evidence and may include the perpetrator; it should not scope or direct the inquiry.
  • The second line (compliance, risk, legal) usually owns the hotline, the response plan and regulatory notification, and supports remediation.
  • The third line (internal audit) can run or support the investigation, but where the matter involves senior management or a possible failure of internal audit itself, the audit committee should engage independent counsel and forensic advisers who report directly to it.
  • The governing body approves scope, receives findings directly, and decides on disclosure and discipline.

The external auditor sits outside the three lines. Auditing standards on fraud (see Auditing Standards and Fraud) require the auditor to respond to identified fraud risks, and in a listed-company setting a credible allegation will usually have to be communicated to the auditor. Weaknesses in the model, such as lines that are not actually independent of one another, are themselves a standard subject of post-incident review.

8 Reporting, self-disclosure and remediation

The end product depends on the audience. A privileged oral briefing to a board is common where litigation is likely; a written report suited to disclosure may be prepared for a regulator, insurer or court. Principles for the written report are covered in Report Writing: findings are stated separately from opinions, each factual statement is tied to an exhibit, limitations and unexamined areas are disclosed, and alternative hypotheses that were tested and rejected are recorded.

Self-disclosure. Whether to disclose to authorities is a legal decision informed by the investigation. In the United States the Department of Justice announced, on 10 March 2026, its first department-wide corporate enforcement policy for criminal matters (other than antitrust), under which a company that voluntarily self-discloses, fully cooperates, timely and appropriately remediates, and has no aggravating circumstances can qualify for a declination; the Criminal Division had issued a revised policy in May 2025.[5] The DOJ's Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs, updated in September 2024, directs prosecutors to ask, among other things, how an organisation investigates misconduct, whether it applies lessons learned and whether it incentivises internal reporting.[6] A well-documented investigation is therefore evidence of programme effectiveness as well as a means of finding facts. Where the organisation is an issuer, the SEC's analogous credit for cooperation similarly rewards prompt, thorough internal reviews, as in the independent review commissioned by Nortel's audit committee after its first restatement in 2003, discussed in the case studies.[7]

Remediation. The investigation concludes with a closing memo and a remediation plan: recovery actions (insurance, civil claims, restitution; see Asset Tracing), discipline and referral decisions, control redesign mapped to the bypass found in section 5, a root-cause statement, and a monitoring plan with owners and dates. Retaliation protection for the originating whistleblower should be confirmed in writing. Lessons from the control-track timeline feed back into the next fraud risk assessment.

Investigators also carry their own risks: prolonged exposure to this work affects practitioners (Forensic Practitioner Burnout), and the reliability of their methods is itself under scrutiny in courts (Daubert Standard, Forensic Validity Crisis). Planning documents are the first line of defence on both counts.
