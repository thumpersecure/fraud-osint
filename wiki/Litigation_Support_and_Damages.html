Litigation Support and Damages

From the Fraud-OSINT Wiki, the free forensic-accounting & OSINT reference
Litigation Support & Damages
RolesConsulting expert; testifying expert; special master or receiver's accountant
Core questionWhat are the plaintiff's damages, caused by whom, and where did the money go?
Federal rulesFRCP 26(a)(2)(B), 26(b)(4)(D), 64; FRE 702
Key damages casesStory Parchment (1931); Comcast v. Behrend (2013)
Key remedies caseGrupo Mexicano v. Alliance Bond Fund (1999)
Tracing rulesLowest intermediate balance; pro rata; FIFO (Clayton's Case)

Litigation support is the application of accounting, financial and investigative skills to disputes. A forensic accountant may quantify damages (what the plaintiff lost), measure a fraud's size, trace misappropriated money, or evaluate another side's expert. The work sits where forensic accounting meets procedure: the numbers must be right, but they also must be admissible, explainable to a lay fact-finder, and tied to a legal theory. Admissibility standards are covered in Daubert Standard and courtroom practice in Evidence and Testimony; this article concentrates on the substantive methods.

1 Roles and engagement structure

A consulting expert advises counsel and is generally shielded from discovery by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(4)(D) absent exceptional circumstances; work product flows through the attorney (the Kovel structure, commonly used to protect accountants assisting lawyers). A testifying expert must serve a written report under Rule 26(a)(2)(B) containing a complete statement of all opinions and their basis, the facts or data considered, exhibits, qualifications including publications from the preceding ten years, a list of cases in which the expert testified in the previous four years, and compensation. Drafts and attorney communications are protected only in part (Rule 26(b)(4)(B)–(C)), so the expert's file must be organized on the assumption it will be read by an adversary. Ethical constraints, independence, and conflicts are discussed in Forensic Accountant Ethics, and report structure in Report Writing.

2 Damages concepts

  • But-for world. Damages compare the plaintiff's actual position to the position it would have occupied but for the wrongful act. Every method is a way of constructing that counterfactual.
  • Fact versus amount. Under Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), the fact of damage must be established with reasonable certainty, but the amount may be estimated with reasonable approximation once a wrong is shown, particularly where the defendant's conduct created the uncertainty.
  • Model fit. In class actions, Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27 (2013), requires that a damages model measure only damages attributable to the theory of liability that survives. A model that cannot isolate the harm from the wrong is vulnerable.
  • Causation, foreseeability and mitigation. Damages must be caused by the defendant; the plaintiff must take reasonable steps to limit loss, and avoided costs are deducted.
  • Time value. Past losses may carry prejudgment interest (rate set by statute, contract or court discretion); future losses are discounted to present value at a rate reflecting risk.

3 Lost-profits methodologies with a worked example

MethodHow the but-for is builtTypical evidence
Before-and-afterPlaintiff's own pre-wrong performance projected forwardHistorical financials, trend analysis
Yardstick / benchmarkComparable firms or plaintiff's other unitsIndustry data, competitor filings
Market-shareShare of a growing or stable marketMarket studies, sales data
Direct (contract/customer)Specific lost orders or contractsBids, pipelines, customer testimony

Lost profit is lost revenue minus the incremental (avoided) costs, not net income and not revenue. Worked example (illustrative): a distributor's but-for revenue is $2,000,000 per year after a competitor's tortious interference, and actual revenue is $1,200,000. Lost revenue is $800,000. Costs that vary with sales are 55% ($440,000 avoided); fixed costs are unchanged. Annual lost profit is $800,000 − $440,000 = $360,000. Over three future years at an 8% discount rate, using an end-of-year convention, present value is $360,000 × [1 − 1.08−3] / 0.08 = $360,000 × 2.5771 = about $927,800. Sensitivities are then shown: if the variable-cost ratio were 65%, annual lost profit would be $280,000 and the present value about $721,600. The defence expert will test the growth assumption, the cost behavior, and whether other causes (a recession, a lost key employee) explain part of the revenue shortfall; the plaintiff's expert should regress or otherwise control for those factors. Business valuation overlaps when the wrong destroys enterprise value rather than income.

4 Quantifying fraud losses

4.1 Specific identification

Where records survive, the loss is the sum of identified fraudulent transactions. Procedure: (1) obtain the full ledger or bank population and confirm completeness; (2) code each suspect transaction to source documents (invoice, approval, receiving record, bank item); (3) separate fraudulent from legitimate transactions with the same payee; (4) deduct value actually received; (5) add consequential components the law allows. Example: payments to a sham vendor total $412,600 over 31 months; the company can document goods of $58,000 actually received. Gross diversion is $412,600, and net direct loss is $354,600; investigation cost (say $46,000), interest and any restitution received are then layered on in separate lines so the court can accept or reject each.

4.2 Sampling and extrapolation

When the population is large, test a statistical sample and extrapolate with a confidence interval. Suppose 10,000 invoices were paid to a suspect vendor group; a random sample of 200 shows 14 fraudulent ones (7.0%), averaging $3,100. The standard error is about √(0.07 × 0.93 / 200) = 1.8 percentage points, so a 95% interval for the rate is roughly 3.5% to 10.5%, or about 346 to 1,054 invoices; the point estimate is 700 invoices, or $2,170,000, and the interval runs from about $1.07 million to $3.27 million (ignoring a finite-population correction). Experts should disclose the sampling frame, randomization method and interval, since a bare point estimate invites a Daubert challenge.

4.3 Indirect methods

When books are destroyed or the fraudster controlled them, income can be reconstructed indirectly—the net worth method, cash-expenditure and bank-deposit methods—by comparing known income sources to assets accumulated and money spent. These establish unexplained wealth but need corroboration to attribute it to the defendant's fraud. Pattern evidence from data analytics (see Fraud Detection Analytics) can help define the scheme's boundaries, and occupational fraud loss statistics provide context but are not a substitute for case-specific calculations.

4.4 Reconstructing the scheme period

Determine the earliest and latest affected dates by testing before and after the first known fraudulent item: take the vendor's first invoice, the date of the control change, and the date of the last payment. Unexplained margins (for example gross margin falling from 41% to 33% in exactly the scheme period while sales are stable) can help cross-check the extrapolated loss.

5 Tracing in civil asset recovery

To recover specific property or impose a constructive trust, a claimant must identify the fraud proceeds in the defendant's hands, even after they have been transferred, commingled or converted. Tracing is an equitable exercise with several competing conventions when proceeds mix with the wrongdoer's own money:

  • Lowest intermediate balance rule (LIBR). The claimant's traceable interest can never exceed the lowest balance reached after the deposit, because money spent is presumed to be the wrongdoer's own first; later deposits of the wrongdoer's own funds do not replenish the trust. This is the most claimant-friendly of the common rules for what remains in the account, and it is reflected in the Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 59.
  • Pro rata (proportionate) tracing. Each withdrawal is treated as drawn proportionally from the account's mixed contents; used, for example, in English law in Barlow Clowes where multiple victims share a pooled fund.
  • First in, first out (FIFO). From Devaynes v Noble (Clayton's Case) (1816): withdrawals are treated as drawn from the earliest deposits. It is often used to allocate among innocent investors in a Ponzi scheme; see Cunningham v. Brown, 265 U.S. 1 (1924), the original Ponzi receivership.

Worked example (illustrative). An account holds $20,000 of the defendant's own money. On 1 March $100,000 of fraud proceeds is deposited (balance $120,000). On 10 March $90,000 is withdrawn for a vehicle (balance $30,000). On 20 March the defendant deposits $40,000 of salary (balance $70,000). On 25 March $50,000 is withdrawn for a down payment (balance $20,000).

RuleFraud funds remaining in accountReasoning
LIBR$20,000Lowest balance after 1 March is $20,000; later deposits do not restore the claim
FIFO$0First withdrawal consumes the $20,000 own money then $70,000 of fraud funds; the second consumes the remaining $30,000 of fraud funds plus $20,000 of salary
Pro rataabout $7,143Fraud share 83.3% on 10 March: $75,000 spent, $25,000 left; share of $70,000 balance is 35.7%; $17,857 spent on 25 March; $7,143 left

The same tracing also identifies the vehicle and the down payment as products of the fraud, which may give the claimant a proprietary claim in those assets (and any appreciation) rather than merely a personal claim. The forensic accountant produces a day-by-day ledger of the account, a source-and-application schedule, and a flow chart for each commingled account, with the rule applied stated explicitly because the court, not the accountant, chooses the legal rule. For the investigative side of finding accounts and assets see Asset Tracing, Cryptocurrency Tracing and Money Laundering.

6 Prejudgment remedies

Because defendants often dissipate assets before judgment, plaintiffs seek early security. Rule 64 of the Federal Rules lets a federal court use the state-law remedies available for seizing property to secure satisfaction of a judgment, such as attachment, garnishment and replevin. State statutes vary; some, like Connecticut's prejudgment remedy statute (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-278a et seq.), require a hearing and a finding of probable cause that the plaintiff will prevail. In contrast, a federal court's authority to issue a preliminary injunction freezing assets in a purely money-damages action is narrow after Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. v. Alliance Bond Fund, Inc., 527 U.S. 308 (1999), which held that such relief was not available in the traditional equity jurisdiction of the federal courts where the plaintiff claims no lien or equitable interest in the assets. The decision leaves room where the plaintiff asserts an equitable claim (constructive trust, or tracing of identifiable proceeds), which is one reason tracing evidence matters at the very start. Other tools include receiverships, lis pendens, and, in common-law jurisdictions outside the United States, freezing injunctions (Mareva orders). Government actions use parallel routes: civil and criminal forfeiture and restitution under statutes such as 18 U.S.C. §§ 981 and 3663A. The expert's declaration at this stage usually presents a preliminary tracing schedule and an estimate of dissipation risk (rapid transfers, offshore wires, conversions to crypto).

7 Critiquing the other side's expert

A rebuttal expert checks: whether the but-for scenario relies on unsupported growth rates; whether avoided costs were ignored; whether double counting occurred (lost profit and lost enterprise value for the same period); whether the discount rate matches the risk of the cash flows; whether the extrapolation sample was random; and whether data were cherry-picked. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702 as amended in December 2023, the proponent must show it is more likely than not that the opinion meets the reliability requirements, strengthening such challenges. Cross-examination preparation is covered in Evidence and Testimony; the reliability problems of forensic disciplines more generally appear in Forensic Validity Crisis.
