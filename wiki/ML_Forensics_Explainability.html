Machine Learning in Digital Forensics and the Explainability Problem

From the Fraud-OSINT Wiki, the free forensic-accounting & OSINT reference

Machine learning (ML) in digital forensics refers to the application of supervised, unsupervised, and deep-learning techniques to the acquisition, triage, and analysis of digital evidence — including malware classification, network-traffic analysis, and large-scale artifact prioritization. It has emerged as a response to what researchers term the "digital forensics data volume problem": investigators facing terabytes of disk images, logs, and network captures that cannot feasibly be reviewed by hand within case timelines. At the same time, ML's growing role in producing conclusions that may be offered as evidence has collided with a legal admissibility framework — built around the Daubert standard and Federal Rule of Evidence 702 — that demands methods be testable, reproducible, and explainable. This tension between black-box predictive accuracy and courtroom-grade transparency is often called the "explainability problem," and it is among the most consequential open questions facing digital forensics as a discipline.

1 The Data Volume Problem

Digital forensic investigations have long been constrained by the sheer scale of the evidence they must process. A single seized device, cloud account, or network capture can generate terabytes of disk images, system logs, and packet data, far exceeding what a human examiner can manually review within the time constraints of an active case. This "data volume problem" is the starting point for Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman's 2020 survey, "Machine learning for digital forensics: Techniques and applications," published in Future Generation Computer Systems (vol. 107, pp. 278-288). The paper frames ML not as a novelty but as a structural necessity: without automated triage and classification, examiners cannot keep pace with the volume of material modern investigations generate, whether that material is a corporate breach investigation, a malware outbreak, or — as discussed further below — a large corpus of trafficking-related online content.

This same volume pressure recurs throughout digital forensics scholarship discussed elsewhere in this wiki. Cloud and Internet-of-Things (IoT) forensics compound the problem further, since evidence sources multiply across providers, devices, and formats, a challenge documented in Dubey, Bhatt, and Negi's 2023 review of digital forensics techniques and trends. The interoperability standards discussed in Data Standards for Cyber-Investigation — the Unified Cyber Ontology (UCO), the Cyber-investigation Analysis Standard Expression (CASE), and the process-oriented WANDA schema — exist in part to make automated, cross-tool analysis of this volume of evidence tractable and consistent, which is the same underlying motivation that drives ML adoption in forensic triage.

2 Survey of ML Applications in Digital Forensics

Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman (2020) survey ML techniques — spanning supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and deep learning — as applied across several forensic sub-domains:

  • Malware analysis and classification. ML models are trained to distinguish malicious from benign code, classify malware families, and detect novel or obfuscated variants that signature-based detection would miss.
  • Network traffic and intrusion forensic analysis. ML is applied to identify anomalous traffic patterns, reconstruct attack timelines, and flag intrusion indicators within large volumes of packet-capture and log data.
  • File and artifact triage and prioritization. Rather than requiring an examiner to manually review every recovered file or log entry, ML models can rank or classify artifacts by forensic relevance, directing scarce examiner attention to the material most likely to matter to a case.
  • Pattern recognition across large evidence datasets. More broadly, ML techniques are used to surface patterns — recurring behaviors, connections between entities, anomalous transactions — that would be difficult for a human reviewer to detect manually across a large corpus.

These same categories of technique are directly relevant to forensic-accounting and fraud-detection work: the anomaly-detection and classification methods surveyed by Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman are structurally the same techniques applied to transaction-level fraud detection, financial-statement anomaly analysis, and large-scale open-source intelligence (OSINT) data triage — the intersection point between digital forensics and financial-crime investigation discussed at length in this wiki's forensic-accounting material.

3 The Explainability and Reproducibility Problem

Alongside its survey of applications, Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman (2020) explicitly identify a set of unresolved challenges specific to applying ML in a forensic, as opposed to general cybersecurity, context. Chief among these is the requirement for explainability and reproducibility of ML-driven conclusions in order for those conclusions to be legally admissible. This is a materially different bar than ordinary cybersecurity or commercial machine learning, where a model's practical accuracy is often sufficient justification for its deployment regardless of whether a human can fully trace its internal reasoning.

The core difficulty is that the most accurate contemporary ML techniques — particularly deep neural networks — are frequently "black boxes": models whose internal weightings and decision logic are not readily interpretable by a human examiner, even though their outputs may be highly accurate on held-out test data. In a forensic setting, this creates a direct conflict. A model may correctly flag a file as malware, a transaction as fraudulent, or a communication as evidence of trafficking activity with high statistical reliability, yet be unable to state why it reached that conclusion in terms a court, opposing counsel, or an independent examiner could evaluate, test, or reproduce. Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman note this tension between black-box deep-learning accuracy and the transparency required for expert testimony as a defining, unresolved methodological problem for the field.

This concern does not exist in isolation from the broader digital forensics validity literature. Reproducibility and explainability are not new demands invented for ML; they echo the foundational-validity requirements long applied to other forensic disciplines, and they are formally embedded in international process standards such as ISO/IEC 27042:2015, which requires that digital evidence analysis be conducted with continuity, validity, reproducibility, and repeatability, and that sufficient information be recorded about each analytical process to permit independent review. An ML pipeline that cannot document how it arrived at a conclusion fails this standard regardless of its raw accuracy.

4 Legal Admissibility: Daubert and FRE 702

The explainability problem is inseparable from the legal test United States courts apply to determine whether expert or scientific evidence — including ML-derived conclusions offered through an expert witness — may be admitted at all. The Daubert standard, established in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), assigns the trial judge a gatekeeping role, requiring an assessment of whether the underlying reasoning or methodology is scientifically valid, considering factors such as whether the technique can be and has been tested, whether it has been subjected to peer review and publication, its known or potential error rate, and its general acceptance in the relevant field.

Daubert's requirements are codified in Federal Rule of Evidence 702, which — as amended effective December 1, 2023 — requires the proponent of expert testimony to show it is more likely than not that the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data, is the product of reliable principles and methods, and reflects a reliable application of those principles and methods to the facts of the case. The 2023 amendment's emphasis on "reliable application," rather than merely being "based on" reliable methods, is squarely relevant to ML-derived forensic conclusions: an examiner cannot simply assert that a model is generally accurate; the examiner must show that the model was reliably applied to the specific evidence at hand, in a way that can be tested and reviewed.

An ML classifier that cannot articulate which features drove a given prediction, or that cannot be independently re-run to reproduce the same result on the same input, is poorly positioned to satisfy this standard. A known or knowable error rate — one of the enumerated Daubert factors — is likewise difficult to establish for a model whose failure modes are not well characterized, or whose training data does not reflect the population of evidence it is later applied to in casework. This places ML-based forensic tools in the same posture the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST) found several traditional pattern-matching disciplines to occupy in its 2016 report: potentially useful, but not yet demonstrated to meet the foundational-validity bar that legal admissibility requires. Digital forensics as a field is, in effect, being asked to avoid repeating a mistake other forensic disciplines have already been criticized for making — deploying a technique in the courtroom before its scientific validity has been rigorously established.

5 Designing for Auditability: The Integrated Gradients Example

A concrete illustration of how researchers have attempted to design ML systems that meet — rather than evade — the explainability demand comes from the domain of trafficking-detection natural language processing (NLP). Rodriguez Perez and Rivas's 2023 paper, "Combatting Human Trafficking in the Cyberspace" (arXiv:2311.13118), presents a methodology for building Transformer-based models to perform two tasks: Human Trafficking Risk Prediction (HTRP) and Organized Activity Detection (OAD), trained on pseudo-labeled datasets generated with minimal human supervision.

What distinguishes this work from a purely accuracy-optimized classifier is its explicit interpretability framework, built on Integrated Gradients — a technique for attributing a model's output to the specific input features (in this case, elements of text) that most influenced it. Rather than returning an opaque risk score, the framework is designed to give investigators an auditable basis for understanding why a given piece of content was flagged, so that a human analyst can review, prioritize, and — critically — challenge or validate the model's reasoning rather than accepting its output on faith. This design choice is directly responsive to the explainability requirements Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman identify as a general challenge for forensic ML, and it reflects the same underlying instinct as ISO/IEC 27042's demand that analytical processes be documented well enough to permit independent review.

The interpretability design also responds to a separate, more foundational critique of automated and heuristic-based trafficking detection. Volodko, Cockbain, and Kleinberg's 2020 study of 430 job advertisements found that 98.4 percent contained at least one commonly cited "trafficking indicator," with only weak and inconsistent statistical relationships between advertisement characteristics and indicator counts — a finding that many widely used red-flag indicators are in fact commonplace features of the legitimate low-wage labor market, not reliable signals of trafficking. This is a caution directly relevant to Human Trafficking Finance investigations: a black-box or keyword-based detection system risks reproducing and automating this over-inclusive indicator problem at scale, flagging enormous volumes of legitimate content as suspicious. An interpretable model that exposes its reasoning gives investigators the means to catch and correct exactly this kind of systematic over-flagging, rather than discovering it only after acting on a large volume of false positives.

6 Adversarial Evasion and Data Scarcity

Beyond explainability, Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman (2020) identify two further challenges specific to deploying ML in forensic contexts. The first is adversarial evasion: because forensic ML models are often deployed against actors with a direct incentive to avoid detection, sophisticated subjects may deliberately craft inputs — obfuscated malware code, manipulated network traffic, disguised communications — designed to fool a known or suspected classifier. This mirrors the broader anti-forensic techniques documented elsewhere in the digital forensics literature, such as cryptographic obfuscation, IP and MAC address spoofing, and other measures used to hinder investigators, and it means a forensic ML model's real-world reliability cannot be assumed to match its performance on a static test dataset once adversaries begin adapting to it.

The second challenge is training-data scarcity and imbalance for rare evidence types. Forensically significant events — a specific malware family, a rare fraud pattern, an unusual trafficking-recruitment technique — are by definition uncommon relative to the general population of data a model might encounter, making it difficult to assemble large, representative, well-labeled training sets. This scarcity problem is part of why Rodriguez Perez and Rivas (2023) turned to pseudo-labeled dataset generation with minimal human supervision, a workaround that itself introduces additional questions about the reliability and potential bias of the resulting training data.

7 Standards and Process Representation

The explainability problem intersects with the broader technical-standards landscape covered in Data Standards for Cyber-Investigation. CASE, the dominant ontology-based standard for representing cyber-investigation data, has been characterized in the academic literature as "artefact-oriented": it can richly describe what a digital artifact is and where it came from, but it lacks strong expressivity for documenting the algorithmic processing steps — such as an ML model's internal decision path — needed to meet legal reliability standards. WANDA, by contrast, was designed as a process-oriented standard specifically in response to Daubert-era criticisms, tracing human-generated versus machine-generated annotations and documenting objective measurements for algorithm evaluation. Researchers have proposed integrating a WANDA-based module into CASE precisely to close this gap — so that a forensic data standard could represent not only an ML-flagged artifact, but the reasoning process that produced the flag. As of the proposing research, this integration remains a proposal rather than an implemented standard, underscoring that the tooling needed to make ML forensic conclusions fully auditable is still under active development.

8 Outlook

The trajectory Alazab, Tang, and Venkatraman describe in 2020 — ML addressing an intractable data-volume problem while raising an unresolved explainability problem — remains the central framing for ML's role in digital forensics. The same tension recurs across adjacent domains covered in Emerging Trends: cloud and IoT forensics multiply the volume and heterogeneity of evidence that automated triage must handle, and financial-crime technology platforms increasingly deploy AI-driven, cross-institution analysis at a scale no human team could replicate manually. In each case, the same unresolved question recurs — how to reconcile a model's practical accuracy with the transparency a legal proceeding, or a rigorous investigator, requires before relying on its output.

Work such as Rodriguez Perez and Rivas's Integrated Gradients framework demonstrates that designing for auditability from the outset is possible, but such designs remain the exception rather than the default in forensic ML tooling, and no consensus standard yet exists for what level of explainability a forensic ML system must provide to satisfy Daubert-style scrutiny. Until such standards mature, any AI-assisted tool adopted for digital forensic or fraud-investigation work should be evaluated against the reliability and reproducibility bar these frameworks describe before its output is treated as dispositive in a legal or high-stakes investigative context.
