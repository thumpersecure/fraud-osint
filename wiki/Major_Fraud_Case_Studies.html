Major Financial Fraud Case Studies

From the Fraud-OSINT Wiki, the free forensic-accounting & OSINT reference

See also: Case Studies (general fraud typologies and shorter examples), Financial Statement Fraud, Fraud Triangle, and Daubert Standard (the legal framework governing expert testimony in several of these prosecutions).

This article surveys ten landmark, adjudicated or formally settled financial frauds spanning roughly two decades, from the collapse of Enron Corporation in 2001 to the 2024 conviction of Archegos Capital Management's Bill Hwang. Each case is treated as a named, citable precedent: the underlying fraud mechanism, the dollar figures established in court or regulatory filings, the individuals held responsible, and the ultimate legal outcome. Together these cases illustrate the recurring mechanisms of large-scale financial fraud — off-balance-sheet entities, expense mischaracterization, fabricated assets, auditor and gatekeeper failure, and outright misappropriation of customer or investor funds — and the institutional responses (Sarbanes-Oxley, expanded SEC enforcement units, criminal sentencing guidelines) that followed. This article functions as the "named landmark cases" companion to the general Case Studies page; readers seeking broader fraud typologies, smaller or non-adjudicated examples, and international schemes not covered here should consult that article instead.

1 Enron: Special Purpose Entities and Gatekeeper Failure

Enron Corporation, once the seventh-largest U.S. company by reported revenue, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on December 2, 2001, after disclosures that its reported profits and balance-sheet strength had been fabricated for years through systematic misuse of off-balance-sheet special purpose entities (SPEs) — legally separate entities created to hold assets or liabilities off a parent company's books. Chief Financial Officer Andrew Fastow engineered a network of these entities, including Chewco, LJM and LJM2, RADR, and the Southampton and Cuiaba transactions, to hide debt and inflate earnings while Fastow personally profited from managing them.[1]

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) brought a civil fraud action against Fastow in October 2002; he settled for a 10-year sentence and more than $23 million in penalties. The SEC later charged Chairman/CEO Kenneth Lay, CEO Jeffrey Skilling, and Chief Accounting Officer Richard Causey, alleging Lay profited more than $90 million from stock sales made while he possessed material non-public information about the company's deteriorating condition. Auditor Arthur Andersen was criminally convicted of obstruction of justice for shredding Enron-related documents; that conviction was later overturned by the U.S. Supreme Court, though the firm had already collapsed as a going concern.[2]

The definitive scholarly account is Healy and Palepu's 2003 peer-reviewed analysis, "The Fall of Enron," published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives. The paper examines why capital-market "gatekeepers" — external auditors, financial analysts, credit-rating agencies, and the board of directors — failed to detect or flag the fraud despite red flags present in Enron's public filings. Healy and Palepu attribute the systemic failure to incentive misalignment: Arthur Andersen's auditor independence was compromised by lucrative consulting-fee income from the same client it audited; analyst independence was compromised by investment-banking relationships; and board members held financial ties to Enron that should have prompted recusal or heightened scrutiny. Their reform recommendations anticipated provisions later enacted in the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, which restricted auditor-provided consulting services and mandated new corporate governance controls. This gatekeeper-failure framework is a foundational reference for understanding how the same pattern recurred in later cases discussed below, notably Lehman Brothers and Wirecard. See Financial Statement Fraud for the general mechanics of earnings manipulation via off-balance-sheet structures.

2 WorldCom: The $7.2 Billion Line-Cost Capitalization Fraud

WorldCom was charged by the SEC on June 27, 2002, the day after the telecommunications company disclosed it would restate five quarters of financial results. The accounting fraud, initially estimated at $3.8 billion, was ultimately found to total approximately $7.2 billion in overstated income from at least 1999 through the first quarter of 2002.[3]

The core mechanism, orchestrated by CFO Scott D. Sullivan together with controller David Myers and accounting staff Betty Vinson, Troy Normand, and Buford Yates, involved improperly capitalizing ordinary operating expenses — specifically "line costs," the fees WorldCom paid to lease network capacity from other telecommunications carriers — as capital expenditures rather than immediate operating expenses. Because capitalized costs are depreciated over years rather than expensed immediately, this reclassification spread the true cost across future reporting periods, artificially inflating reported earnings in the period the cost was actually incurred.

The scheme unraveled after WorldCom's internal audit team, led by vice president of internal audit Cynthia Cooper, uncovered the improper entries during a routine review — working largely on her own initiative and outside normal reporting channels after encountering resistance. Sullivan pleaded guilty in March 2004 and was sentenced to five years' imprisonment; CEO Bernard Ebbers was convicted separately and sentenced to 25 years. WorldCom's July 2002 Chapter 11 filing was, at the time, the largest bankruptcy in U.S. corporate history, with roughly 20,000 jobs lost and an estimated $180 billion in investor losses; the company later re-emerged as MCI. Cooper's discovery is frequently cited in fraud-examination literature as an example of internal-audit-driven detection, though it remains a comparatively rare detection path relative to tips from whistleblowers generally (see Fraud Triangle for the opportunity and rationalization elements this case illustrates).[3]

3 HealthSouth: Overstatement and Acquittal

The SEC's emergency enforcement action against HealthSouth Corporation and its founder and CEO, Richard M. Scrushy, filed in the Northern District of Alabama on March 19, 2003, alleged the healthcare-services company overstated earnings by at least $1.4 billion between 1999 and 2002 in order to meet Wall Street analyst expectations. By the third quarter of 2002, assets were overstated by at least $800 million — roughly 10 percent — through fictitious contractual adjustments and fabricated asset entries.[4]

Fifteen other HealthSouth executives pleaded guilty in connection with the scheme. Scrushy himself, however, was acquitted on all 36 criminal fraud counts in 2005 — a widely cited example in the forensic-accounting and trial-advocacy literature of a successful reasonable-doubt defense in a white-collar case despite the scale of the underlying fraud and the guilty pleas of numerous subordinates who testified against him. Scrushy was nevertheless later found liable in related civil suits and was separately convicted in an unrelated bribery case. The HealthSouth outcome is frequently contrasted with the WorldCom and Enron prosecutions to illustrate how prosecutorial success in financial-fraud cases can turn heavily on the availability of direct documentary or testimonial evidence tying a top executive personally to specific fraudulent entries, rather than on the scale of the loss alone.

4 Lehman Brothers: Repo 105 and the Valukas Report

Lehman Brothers Holdings filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on September 15, 2008 — still the largest bankruptcy filing in U.S. history, involving more than $600 billion in assets, and a proximate trigger of the broader 2008 global financial crisis. Anton R. Valukas, chairman of the law firm Jenner & Block, was appointed bankruptcy examiner and delivered a nine-volume report of roughly 2,200 pages, publicly released in redacted form on March 11, 2010, and in full on April 14, 2010. The Valukas Report was based on interviews of more than 250 witnesses and review of approximately 35 million pages of documents, and is widely regarded as one of the most thorough forensic financial investigations ever conducted.[5]

The report's central finding concerned an accounting technique nicknamed "Repo 105." A repurchase agreement ("repo") is ordinarily treated as a secured financing — the seller retains the asset on its balance sheet and records a corresponding liability — rather than a true sale. Lehman, however, classified repo transactions in which the collateral was valued at 105 percent or more of the cash received as outright asset sales, allowing it to temporarily remove approximately $50 billion in assets from its balance sheet at each quarter-end before quietly repurchasing the same assets shortly after the reporting date. This improved Lehman's reported leverage ratio from roughly 13.9 to 12.1 at the relevant reporting dates. The Valukas Report found the practice had no legitimate business purpose beyond balance-sheet manipulation, and that senior executives including CEO Richard Fuld were aware or should have been aware of its use and scale.

The report was also sharply critical of Lehman's outside auditor, Ernst & Young, finding the firm was aware of the Repo 105 practice's use and scale but failed to challenge it or to adequately investigate a whistleblower complaint raised internally in June 2008 by senior vice president Matthew Lee, who had flagged the accounting treatment to Lehman's own auditors before the firm's collapse. Lehman reported $41 billion in available liquidity on September 12, 2008 — two days before its collapse — when only roughly $2 billion was in fact truly unencumbered. The case is a frequently cited precedent for auditor-failure analysis alongside Enron and Wirecard, and the Valukas Report itself is treated as a model of forensic document-review methodology at a scale (35 million pages) that anticipated the later use of technology-assisted review and forensic data analytics discussed in Part V of the broader forensic-accounting literature.

5 Bernie Madoff: The $65 Billion Ponzi Scheme

The SEC sued Bernard L. Madoff and his investment advisory firm on December 11, 2008, alleging a securities fraud initially estimated at $50 billion. The scheme was ultimately determined to total roughly $65 billion in fabricated account-statement value, though actual net cash losses to victims — the amount investors put in minus the amount withdrawn before the scheme's collapse — were approximately $17–20 billion.[6]

Federal prosecutors filed an eleven-count Criminal Information against Madoff on March 10, 2009. Madoff pleaded guilty to all eleven counts on March 12, 2009, with no plea agreement, admitting he had run a classic Ponzi scheme — paying returns to earlier investors using money contributed by newer investors, rather than from any genuine investment activity — for decades, while fabricating trade confirmations and account statements to conceal the absence of underlying trading. Judge Denny Chin sentenced Madoff on June 29, 2009 to the statutory maximum of 150 years in federal prison, a figure reached by "stacking" the maximum sentence available for each of the eleven counts consecutively.

The SEC separately charged Madoff's outside auditor, David G. Friehling, of the three-person firm Friehling & Horowitz, CPAs, with securities fraud for issuing unqualified (clean) audit opinions on Madoff's operations without performing meaningful audit procedures. The Friehling prosecution is a foundational case study in auditor-independence failure and the limits of nominal third-party verification, since Madoff's firm had been examined by SEC staff on multiple occasions over the years without the fraud being detected — a fact that later prompted internal SEC reforms to examination procedures. The Madoff case remains the largest Ponzi scheme in history by fabricated value and is the standard reference point whenever the term "Ponzi scheme" is used in fraud-examination contexts; see Fraud Triangle for the rationalization and opportunity dynamics that sustain such schemes over long periods without detection.

6 Theranos: Elizabeth Holmes and Investor Fraud

Elizabeth Holmes, founder and CEO of blood-testing startup Theranos, and Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani, the company's president and chief operating officer, were indicted on charges of conspiracy to commit wire fraud and wire fraud against both investors and patients. The indictment arose from claims that Theranos's proprietary "Edison" devices could run comprehensive laboratory tests from a few drops of finger-stick blood, while the company secretly ran most tests on modified, commercially available third-party analyzers and concealed known accuracy problems from investors, business partners, and patients.[7]

Holmes was convicted on January 3, 2022 of one count of conspiracy and three counts of wire fraud against investors — involving wire transfers totaling more than $140 million — while being acquitted of all patient-fraud counts; the jury was hung on three additional investor-related counts. Balwani was convicted on all twelve counts, spanning both investor and patient fraud, in a separate trial in July 2022. Judge Edward Davila sentenced Holmes on November 18, 2022 to 135 months (11 years, 3 months) in federal prison plus three years of supervised release; both Holmes and Balwani were held jointly and severally liable for restitution of $452,047,268. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals later upheld Holmes's conviction on appeal. The case is a significant precedent for distinguishing investor fraud from consumer/patient fraud within a single scheme, and for illustrating how a jury can convict on some counts while acquitting on closely related counts arising from the same underlying conduct.

7 Wirecard: The Fictitious €1.9 Billion Escrow

Wirecard AG, a German payment-processing company once valued above €24 billion and a constituent of the DAX-30 stock index, collapsed in June 2020 after admitting that €1.9 billion supposedly held in escrow accounts at two Philippine banks — BDO Unibank and Bank of the Philippine Islands — probably "did not exist."[8]

An independent special investigation conducted by KPMG, delivered April 27–28, 2020, concluded the firm was unable to substantiate more than half of Wirecard's reported profits for the 2016–2018 period and could not verify the existence of the customer relationships underlying most of the company's reported third-party acquiring revenue. Wirecard's statutory auditor, Ernst & Young, subsequently refused to sign off on the company's 2019 annual accounts. On June 18, 2020, the cash shortfall was publicly disclosed, and the named Philippine banks immediately and categorically denied ever holding the funds in question. Wirecard filed for insolvency on June 25, 2020, owing creditors roughly €3.5 billion. CEO Markus Braun was arrested and later stood trial in Germany.

The Wirecard case is a landmark in the literature on third-party confirmation fraud — the fabrication of bank or counterparty confirmations that auditors rely on as independent corroboration of reported assets — and on regulatory capture: Germany's financial regulator, BaFin, had banned short-selling of Wirecard stock in February 2019 and pursued market-manipulation allegations against Financial Times journalists whose years of investigative reporting had raised early questions about the company's accounting, rather than investigating the company itself. The case sits alongside Enron and Lehman Brothers as a primary example of statutory-auditor and regulator failure to act on available warning signs. See Financial Statement Fraud for the general category of fabricated-asset schemes this case exemplifies.

8 FTX: Sam Bankman-Fried and Customer Fund Misappropriation

Samuel Bankman-Fried founded the cryptocurrency exchange FTX and its affiliated trading firm, Alameda Research. Both entities collapsed in November 2022 amid revelations that FTX had improperly used customer deposits to cover Alameda's trading losses and to fund lavish personal spending, political donations, and venture investments, through a secret "backdoor" mechanism that let Alameda draw on FTX customer deposits without the risk controls applied to other trading accounts on the platform.[9]

Following a one-month jury trial, Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts against him — including wire fraud and conspiracy to commit wire fraud, securities fraud, and commodities fraud, plus money-laundering conspiracy — on November 2, 2023. Prosecutors established that he misappropriated billions of dollars in customer funds, defrauding FTX investors of more than $1.7 billion and Alameda's lenders of more than $1.3 billion. Judge Lewis A. Kaplan sentenced him on March 28, 2024 to 25 years in federal prison, plus three years of supervised release and $11 billion in forfeiture, increasing the applicable sentencing guidelines range after finding that Bankman-Fried had perjured himself at trial and had obstructed justice. The FTX case is the primary modern precedent for customer-fund commingling and misappropriation in the cryptocurrency-exchange context, distinguishing it from the securities-fraud mechanisms used in the corporate-accounting cases above.

9 Binance: The $4.3 Billion AML Settlement

Binance Holdings Limited, operator of the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, pleaded guilty on November 21, 2023 to conspiracy to violate the Bank Secrecy Act, operating an unlicensed money-transmitting business, and violating the International Emergency Economic Powers Act by permitting U.S. persons and users in sanctioned jurisdictions to transact on its platform.[10]

Unlike the other cases in this article, the Binance matter is primarily an anti-money-laundering (AML) and sanctions-compliance enforcement action rather than a financial-statement or investor-disclosure fraud. Court filings detailed years of willful failure to maintain an effective AML program: Binance did not register as a money-services business, allowed transactions involving users in sanctioned jurisdictions including Iran, and failed to file Suspicious Activity Reports on more than 100,000 transactions tied to designated terrorist organizations, including Hamas, Al Qaeda, and ISIS. Binance agreed to pay $4.3 billion in combined penalties across the Department of Justice, the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control and Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — the largest corporate criminal resolution of its kind ever imposed on a cryptocurrency company — and agreed to multi-year independent compliance monitoring. Founder and CEO Changpeng "CZ" Zhao personally pleaded guilty to a Bank Secrecy Act violation and resigned as CEO on the same day. The Binance case is the primary reference point in this article's set for the statutory AML/sanctions framework, distinct from the accounting-fraud mechanisms discussed above.

10 Archegos Capital Management: Hidden Leverage via Swaps

Archegos Capital Management, a family office run by former Tiger Asia manager Sung Kook "Bill" Hwang, collapsed within roughly one week in late March 2021 after concentrated, highly leveraged positions built through total-return swaps — synthetic derivative contracts that gave Archegos massive economic exposure to underlying stocks, such as ViacomCBS and various Chinese American depositary receipts, without Archegos directly owning or publicly disclosing the shares — triggered margin calls the firm could not meet.[11]

The unwind cost global investment banks, including Credit Suisse, Nomura, and Morgan Stanley, collectively more than $10 billion, and was a significant contributing factor to Credit Suisse's subsequent financial instability. Hwang and Archegos's chief financial officer, Patrick Halligan, were indicted on charges including racketeering conspiracy, securities fraud, market manipulation, and wire fraud, alleging a scheme to manipulate market prices and to deceive at least nine investment banks and brokerages about the true size and leverage of Archegos's swap-based positions. Because total-return swaps did not, at the time, trigger the same public disclosure requirements as direct equity ownership, Archegos was able to build effectively hidden, highly concentrated leverage across multiple counterparties simultaneously, none of which had full visibility into the others' exposure. Following a nine-week jury trial, Hwang was convicted on 10 counts in July 2024; Judge Alvin Hellerstein sentenced him on November 20, 2024 to 18 years in federal prison. The Archegos case is the primary modern precedent for derivative-based leverage concealment and multi-counterparty disclosure gaps, distinct from the balance-sheet and asset-fabrication mechanisms used in the earlier corporate cases.

11 Cross-Cutting Patterns

Read together, these ten cases fall into several recurring mechanism categories rather than a single template:

  • Off-balance-sheet concealment: Enron's special purpose entities and Lehman Brothers' Repo 105 transactions both used structured transactions to move liabilities or assets off the reporting entity's balance sheet at critical reporting dates.
  • Expense/revenue mischaracterization: WorldCom's capitalization of ordinary line costs and HealthSouth's fictitious contractual adjustments both involved reclassifying or fabricating ordinary accounting entries to inflate reported earnings.
  • Fabricated assets or counterparties: Wirecard's fictitious escrow accounts and Madoff's fabricated trade confirmations both relied on third parties (banks, brokers) that either did not exist as described or were never adequately verified by an independent auditor.
  • Direct misappropriation of customer or investor funds: Madoff and FTX both, at core, took money entrusted by investors or customers and used it for purposes other than what was represented, though through very different institutional structures (an investment advisory firm versus a cryptocurrency exchange).
  • Regulatory/compliance failure rather than accounting fraud: Binance's case is distinct from the others in that the underlying conduct was a sustained AML and sanctions-compliance failure rather than financial-statement manipulation.
  • Hidden leverage via derivatives: Archegos illustrates a mechanism — synthetic exposure via swaps — that did not require falsifying any financial statement at all, only exploiting a disclosure gap across multiple counterparties.

A second recurring theme is gatekeeper failure: in Enron, Lehman Brothers, and Wirecard specifically, the statutory external auditor (Arthur Andersen, Ernst & Young, and Ernst & Young respectively) either had access to information suggesting fraud and failed to act on it, or failed to perform procedures adequate to detect fabricated assets and counterparties. Healy and Palepu's 2003 gatekeeper-failure framework, developed specifically for Enron, is applicable with only minor modification to both later cases. Whistleblowers and internal actors also recur as a detection mechanism distinct from external audit: WorldCom's fraud was uncovered by its own internal audit team under Cynthia Cooper, and Lehman's Repo 105 practice had been flagged internally by Matthew Lee before the firm's collapse, though in Lehman's case the warning was not acted upon.

Sentencing outcomes across the set also vary widely and are not simply proportional to dollar losses: Madoff's $65 billion scheme drew a symbolic 150-year sentence, while Hwang's Archegos case, involving more than $10 billion in bank losses, drew 18 years, and HealthSouth's Scrushy — despite a $1.4 billion overstatement and fifteen subordinate guilty pleas — was acquitted outright on all criminal counts. These outcomes reflect the specific evidentiary and legal-standard requirements discussed in Daubert Standard and related expert-testimony admissibility doctrine, since financial-fraud prosecutions typically depend heavily on forensic accounting expert testimony to establish both the fraudulent mechanism and its financial magnitude to a jury.
