Net Worth Method

From the Fraud-OSINT Wiki, the free forensic-accounting & OSINT reference
Net Worth Method
TypeIndirect method of proof
Core identityAssets − Liabilities = Net worth
PurposeEstimate unreported / illegal income
OriginU.S. tax prosecutions (Al Capone, 1931)
RelatedSource-and-application-of-funds method

The net worth method is an indirect method of proof used by forensic accountants, tax investigators, and fraud examiners to estimate a subject's income when direct records of that income are unavailable, incomplete, or concealed. Rather than adding up income directly, the method infers it: if a person's wealth and spending grow by more than their known, legitimate income can account for, the unexplained increase is treated as unreported — and often illegal — income. It is a foundational technique in following the money and sits alongside the analytical methods used to build financial cases.[1]

1 Concept and formula

The method rests on the accounting identity that net worth is what a person owns less what they owe, measured at a point in time:

Assets − Liabilities = Net worth

The investigator constructs a net worth statement at the start and end of a period (typically a tax year) from all identifiable assets — cash, bank balances, real estate, vehicles, investments, business interests — and all liabilities. The increase in net worth over the period is then combined with the subject's living expenses and adjusted for known, legitimate income to isolate the unexplained portion:

Change in net worth
  + Living expenses (non-deductible personal outlays)
  − Known / reported income (and non-taxable receipts)
  = Unexplained (unreported or illegal) income

The logic is that every dollar a person acquires must either be spent, saved (increasing net worth), or used to pay down debt. If the sum of spending and net-worth growth exceeds documented income, the difference had to come from somewhere the subject did not report. A closing net worth of $600,000 against an opening $200,000, plus $150,000 of living expenses, against $180,000 of reported income implies roughly $370,000 of unexplained income for the period. The method is called indirect precisely because it never observes the illicit income directly; it deduces the income's existence and size from its footprints in assets and spending. That indirection is its great strength against subjects who keep no books for their illegal earnings, and also its principal vulnerability, since the conclusion is only as reliable as the completeness of the asset inventory and the accuracy of the starting point.

2 Origin: the Capone case

The net worth method is closely associated with the U.S. prosecution of gangster Al Capone, who was convicted of tax evasion in 1931. Investigators could not document Capone's illegal earnings directly, but they could demonstrate a lifestyle and accumulation of wealth far beyond any income he had declared, establishing that he must have had large unreported income. The approach proved that financial evidence could reach criminals whose underlying crimes were otherwise hard to charge, and it became a template for indirect proof in tax and financial-crime investigations. The U.S. Supreme Court later addressed the method's evidentiary use, requiring investigators to establish an accurate opening net worth ("starting point") and to investigate leads pointing to non-taxable sources of the increase.[2]

3 Use in tax and laundering cases

Indirect methods remain a staple of tax-fraud enforcement and of money-laundering investigations, where the whole purpose of laundering is to obscure the origin of funds and defeat direct tracing. When layered transfers and front businesses frustrate a transaction-by-transaction reconstruction, the net worth method attacks the problem from the outside: it measures the wealth that ultimately lands with the subject regardless of the path it took. It is frequently used to corroborate asset tracing, to support forfeiture of unexplained wealth, and to quantify the proceeds of predicate offenses. Because it relies on comprehensive financial records, it also draws on the same bank, wire, and public-record sources that underpin open-source financial investigation.[3]

4 Source-and-application-of-funds method

A closely related indirect technique is the source-and-application-of-funds method (also called the expenditures method). Instead of comparing net worth at two dates, it lists all applications of funds during the period — every expenditure and increase in assets or decrease in liabilities — and all sources of funds — income and other legitimate receipts, decreases in assets, increases in liabilities. If applications exceed sources, the excess represents funds from an undisclosed source:

Total applications (expenditures + asset increases + debt reductions)
  − Total known sources (reported income + asset decreases + new borrowing)
  = Funds from undisclosed source

The two methods are analytically equivalent and often computed together as a cross-check: the source-and-application approach is frequently easier when a subject spends heavily rather than accumulating assets, while the net worth statement is stronger when wealth is being stockpiled. Both are indirect methods of proof, and both must account for cash on hand, gifts, loans, and inheritances that could innocently explain the gap.

5 Requirements and challenges

Indirect methods are only as sound as their inputs, and defense challenges typically target three points. First, the opening net worth must be established accurately — an understated starting point manufactures phantom income in later years, which is why the "cash hoard" defense (claiming a large undocumented pile of cash existed at the start) is common. Second, investigators must pursue non-taxable and legitimate sources — loans, gifts, inheritances, prior savings, and asset sales — that could explain an increase without wrongdoing; failure to run down such leads undermines the case. Third, living expenses must be reasonably estimated rather than assumed. Well-documented net worth cases therefore lean heavily on primary records and, where necessary, on the subject's own admissions during interviews. Properly built, the method yields a defensible estimate of concealed income; carelessly built, it collapses under a plausible innocent explanation.
